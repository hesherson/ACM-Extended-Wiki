Acute and structural injury

Structural severity: injury history

The lasting, high-water injury grade. It sets the patient's remaining cerebrovascular and autonomic reserve and the pressure requirements for recovery. Severe and critical structural injury remain vulnerable after acute symptoms settle.

Acute severity: active burden

The current secondary injury burden. It can rise with new insults and fall when oxygen delivery, ventilation, ICP and perfusion improve. Stable mild or moderate TBI can resolve toward zero, subject to any herniation injury floor.

Resolved does not mean erased

A concussion can remain in the history without continuing to generate ICP, unconsciousness or deterioration. As acute burden resolves, the ICP target returns toward the normal 10 mmHg baseline, rather than zero.

Both severity values use a 0–1 scale. A fresh injury initializes both. Repeat impact or blast compares incoming severity with the current acute value, while structural severity preserves the greater historical injury grade. A previously resolved injury can therefore become active again.

Assessment priorities

  1. Response and pupils. Reassess equal, reactive, sluggish or fixed pupils alongside sedation and the patient's ability to respond.
  2. Hemorrhage and circulation. Check volume, pressure and perfusion trends. Well saturated but severely depleted blood can still deliver too little oxygen to the brain.
  3. Airway and effective ventilation. Check the chest, delivered breathing support and oxygenation. Ventilator settings alone do not establish adequate ventilation.
  4. Response to treatment. Reassess after positioning, fluids, pressors, osmotherapy or ventilator changes. Rising pressure can represent compensation; a later fall can signal failing vascular control.

Head impact and blast exposure can enter the TBI system. A visible head wound is not required for blast related TBI.

ICP and brain perfusion

Intracranial pressure opposes the pressure supplying the brain. The recovery calculation uses effective brain MAP:

CPPBrain perfusion pressure=Effective MAPMAP used by the brain−ICPPressure inside the skull

Head elevation subtracts a default 6 mmHg from the MAP used by this calculation. The debug overlay currently subtracts ICP from systemic MAP instead, so displayed CPP can differ from the recovery calculation when the head is elevated.

Active injury burden, poor ventilation, altered cerebral blood volume, fluid overload, positive pressure and ventilator dyssynchrony can raise ICP. The burden component uses the greater of baseline ICP and 40 × acute severity; 40 mmHg is not an absolute cap on combined effects. Structural history alone no longer sustains the old severity driven ICP burden.

Pressure changes over time

The base injury rise coefficient is 0.015 per second, scaled by acute severity. The maximum configured CO₂ coefficient is 0.12 per second, scaled by the ventilation disturbance. The ordinary global rise cap is 0.025 mmHg per second and the fall cap is 0.04 mmHg per second. These are coefficients and limits, not a guaranteed patient slope. Procedure effects can be applied separately.

Mild or moderate: stable recovery

Effective MAP 70, ICP 10, CPP 60: the pressure gate passes. With adequate oxygen delivery and ventilation, acute burden can resolve. CPP 70 is not required.

Severe: preserve pressure

Structural 0.75 gives a MAP floor of 72.5. Effective MAP 80 and ICP 14 give CPP 66, passing the 55 minimum. Recovery can occur while reduced reserve remains.

Critical: check both gates

Structural 0.90 gives a MAP floor of 80. At effective MAP 80 and ICP 25, CPP is only 55. MAP and ICP pass, but the critical CPP minimum of 60 does not.

The legacy CPP 70 target and debug CPP warning colors are not universal healing gates. PerfOK reports the pressure predicate only; it does not confirm oxygen delivery, SpO₂ or ventilation. See the debug field reference.

Autoregulation and autonomic tone

Autoreg: control inside the brain

Cerebral autoregulatory integrity describes how well brain vessels adjust to pressure. Mild TBI retains essentially normal control, moderate injury only mild impairment, and severe or critical structural injury progressively less reserve. Secondary injury, exhausted compensation and herniation can reduce it further.

Impaired autoregulation makes low CPP more damaging. High CPP becomes increasingly pressure passive: excessive pressure can add to ICP. Raising pressure indefinitely is not always protective.

Auto: systemic vascular control

Autonomic integrity is the remaining ability to regulate systemic vascular tone. Signed tone shows the direction: positive means vasoconstriction, zero neutral, and negative vasodilation. Tone ranges from −1 to +1 and changes progressively over time.

These are separate integrity states. A historical severe injury can have stable acute symptoms while both retain reduced reserve.

  1. Stable physiology: systemic TBI tone stays near neutral.
  2. ICP rising: sympathetic tone becomes positive and increases peripheral resistance.
  3. Compensated severe pressure: a Cushing response supports MAP through resistance, with associated vital changes.
  4. Reserve failing: tone becomes labile and may swing between constriction and dilation.
  5. Advanced or terminal failure: dominant vasodilation removes pressure support and contributes to falling perfusion.

Vascular failure can appear without the full Cushing pattern. Once autonomic reserve is exhausted, the separate vascular path can alter peripheral resistance without imposing Cushing bradycardia, abnormal breathing or a hypertensive target. Do not wait for all three vital signs to change before reassessing.

What reduced reserve means in the model

Structural autoregulation baselines are 1.00 through mild injury, then interpolate to 0.95 at moderate, 0.70 at severe and 0.35 at critical maximum severity. Autonomic baselines interpolate from 1.00 to 0.97, 0.80 and 0.55 respectively. Acute burden, compensation exhaustion and herniation can push the live values below these baselines; both live integrities are bounded between 0.05 and 1.

With impaired autoregulation, the pressure passive component begins above CPP 70 and scales to its full fraction at 100 mmHg, weighted by lost integrity. Its configured maximum contribution is 8 mmHg. These are internal model thresholds, not an instruction to target CPP 100.

The default compensation budget is 600 seconds. It accumulates only when the pressure predicate fails and the injury is eligible: structural severity at least 0.60, acute severity at least 0.65, or ICP at least 25. Otherwise the budget recovers. A stable, well perfused mild or moderate injury does not exhaust a timer simply because its history remains.

TBI and junctional bleeding

The interaction runs through circulation rather than a fixed TBI bleeding multiplier. These directions describe the custom junctional channel with other inputs held equal; changing MAP, volume, wound control and coagulation still affect actual loss.

Compensation: more resistance

Positive sympathetic tone increases peripheral resistance, tending to reduce junctional flow. This temporary protection does not close the wound.

Failure: less resistance

Negative tone lowers resistance, tending to increase junctional flow. Lost autonomic integrity also weakens the vessel's temporary spasm and compensation.

Remaining compensation ability

25% + (75% × autonomic integrity)

Integrity 1.00 preserves 100% of ordinary compensation. At the equation's zero-integrity endpoint, 25% remains. The current controller stops at 0.05, corresponding to 28.75%.

This scales the existing compensation mechanism, not total blood loss. Signed tone is already carried by systemic peripheral resistance and is not applied a second time in the junctional equation. The combined circulation update debits the blood compartment once. See Hemorrhage & shock for wound control and other loss factors.

Recovery and persistent injury

When all recovery conditions hold, acute severity falls by a default 0.0025 per second toward its injury floor. This is an uninterrupted model rate, not a guaranteed recovery time. Recent hypertonic saline or mannitol can double the rate for 120 seconds; the physiology gates still apply.

The full TBI vital-pattern controller retains nonterminal limits on its HR, MAP and breathing targets. Those limits are not a survival guarantee: the separate vascular path and simultaneous blood loss or hypoxia still matter.

Positioning, treatment and transport

In this game implementation, elevated head positioning blocks new herniation progression and allows the cascade to recede. It also lowers effective brain MAP by 6 mmHg, so reassess pressure gates after positioning.

Hypertonic saline and mannitol can reduce pressure through osmotherapy. Address inadequate circulating volume and perfusion as well. Excessive ventilation can lower ICP while adding ischemic injury; pressure support and ventilation need reassessment rather than indefinite escalation.

When the vehicle evacuation option is enabled, entering a vehicle marks the patient as evacuated for the herniation calculation and pauses cascade progression. This does not erase structural history, established injury or other physiological problems. Vehicle evacuation does not stop an already active stage 3 terminal arrest timer. Continue monitoring during transport.

Progression timing

At default settings, ICP at or above 30 mmHg accumulates time toward a 600-second herniation gate while the head is not elevated. Below that pressure, or with the head elevated, accumulated time falls at half speed. Once the gate is open, progression uses 360-second stage intervals. Pressure control, elevated head positioning and evacuation can interrupt or pause progression. This herniation clock is separate from the compensation budget.
