IV catheter placement

Open the medical menu, select an arm or leg, then choose Establish IV. Apply the band, palpate the target with an empty hand, scrub the site, and select a catheter gauge from the tray. The images below are the mod’s actual 18G catheter frames. Other gauges use the same advance, thread and retract sequence.

Default mouse controls: LMB = left mouse button; MMB = middle mouse button (press the wheel); RMB = right mouse button. SCROLL UP / SCROLL DOWN mean rotate the wheel.

Stages advance every 7 seconds while on screen. The thin line below shows time until the next stage. Use the arrows or stage selector at any time. Slideshow keyboard: LEFT ARROW / RIGHT ARROW, HOME / END. Pause to read or inspect a frame.

Confirm the site after placement

The artwork alone does not establish a successful IV. A missed puncture can still reach the hubbed frame and leave a compromised site. Remove the band and reassess for swelling or poor delivery. Compare actual fluid delivery with the site rules below.

01

Choose IV or IO access

Critical casualty: make early IO part of the access plan. For most critical patients in ACM Extended, treat IO as a routine priority so urgent medication has a route while your best IV sites remain available for blood and fluids. Repeated IV attempts consume time and can leave holes that compromise later sites.

Plan one running bag or Y set per access site. The prepared set menu refuses another running line on an occupied IV or IO. A blood Y set includes its clamped saline reserve, but still occupies one access site. More independent infusions require more usable sites.

1 Does medication need to reach the patient urgently?

Yes: critical or rapidly deteriorating

Establish IO early

Use the available IO procedure to obtain a medication route. Keep the best peripheral IV sites for blood or another infusion that needs its own line.

Why: a difficult peripheral vein should not hold up the whole access plan. IO avoids the peripheral IV leakage model.

No: enough time to choose a vein

Choose a usable IV site deliberately

Match the site to the medication, required flow and vein you can actually palpate. Check previous attempts before inserting.

Why: a functioning smaller catheter is more useful than a larger catheter that misses or leaks.

Then assign a purpose to each access site.

2 Is the medication a vesicant or irritant?

Yes: site integrity matters especially

Favor a functioning IO for urgent pushes

In a critical patient, IO provides a medication path while preserving IV sites for fluids. If using an IV, choose intact anatomy and reassess that exact site for leakage, bruising and pain.

Why: leaked medication can cause local injury and reduce the dose reaching circulation. Moving to an upper IV or an EJ does not make it immune.

No: the catheter still has to work

Choose by flow demand and placement margin

Use a suitable IV for the planned fluid or medication. A lower risk medication can still be lost through a compromised site.

Why: absence of a vesicant label does not prove that the displayed syringe dose reached the patient.

For peripheral IV access, check the limb before choosing a gauge.

3 Has this limb already had a missed or removed IV?

Yes: there may be a downstream leak

Move above the damage, to another limb, or to IO

On a limb, placing below a missed or removed site can send fluid toward the old hole and cause leakage. An intact IV above every compromised site avoids that particular downstream hole rule.

Why: the game tracks the upper, middle and lower tiers and the actual height of the puncture within them. Choosing the same tier alone is not enough.

No: compare vein size and perfusion

Use the vein you can reliably locate

Low pressure narrows the peripheral placement target and weakens the palpation cue. Larger, shallower, less mobile veins are more forgiving.

Why: a larger catheter reduces the placement margin further. IO or EJ may be useful when the peripheral target is poor.

Confirm access, then treat. Remove the constricting band from an IV, select the correct site for the bag or syringe, and reassess actual delivery and patient response. IO does not remove systemic medication effects or every cause of stopped flow.

Choose the gauge for the job and the vein

Lower gauge numbers mean larger catheters. Flow is only one part of the choice: placement accuracy, vein caliber and an intact delivery path determine whether that capacity is useful.

What the game models for this access plan

The early IO recommendation is a gameplay priority for critical casualties. The game does not require every critical patient to receive one before other treatment.

The current delivery path applies vesicant injury to medication that actually leaks at the selected peripheral IV, including upper sites and EJ. IO leakage is not modeled by that path. This does not make IO immune to medication overdose, systemic adverse effects or other access problems.

Calcium chloride, epinephrine, norepinephrine, HTS and mannitol are examples in the vesicant table. Amiodarone, magnesium and esmolol use irritant entries. Calcium gluconate also has a higher threshold entry. Check each medication card and the access site instead of assuming that a medication or proximal site is harmless.

Infusions in PFC and TFC

Continuous medication drips are intended to be used more often during prolonged field care (PFC). Sustained analgesia, sedation and pressure support require repeated monitoring, reliable access and time to manage the bag and clamp.

During tactical field care (TFC), prioritize immediate bleeding control, airway and breathing support, indicated medications, resuscitation and evacuation. A drip should have a clear purpose that justifies the setup and attention it needs. This is a gameplay emphasis, not a ban: blood resuscitation and indicated finite infusions may still be needed during TFC.

“Infusion” includes a finite dose delivered over time. “Continuous drip” describes ongoing delivery adjusted to a sustained treatment goal. Starting an infusion does not guarantee a stable patient or remove the need to reassess. The Joint Trauma System guidelines include separate TCCC and prolonged care references.

Infusion doses and risk ranges

Choose a medication to explore delivery over time, or open its quick reference to compare dose ranges and hazards. Use the mixture workbench for syringe preparation and pressor bags.

Game reference, default settings. There is no single safe dose for every patient. These ranges separate actual rate hazards, current drug effect and accumulated exposure. Where the mod has no defined toxicity cutoff, the guide says so. A reference range is not a guarantee of safety, and a high cutoff is not a target.

Weight examples use 83 kg. Values in mg/min, mcg/min, mg/kg and total mg are different quantities. Any concentration called a proxy is an internal game estimate, not a measured laboratory result.

Amount in the bag

The recipe is what you prepare. A 4 mg norepinephrine bag or a 2.5 g esmolol bag does not mean the patient should receive that amount at once.

Rate reaching the patient

Concentration × admitted flow determines the dose per minute. Leakage, a closed clamp or a blocked route can make the actual dose lower than requested. Use the drip calculator with the final mixture concentration.

Effect still on board

Earlier pushes and drips continue to act. Active-equivalent dose means the amount represented by the current onset and washout weighted drug effect, not simply the total emptied from the bag.

Why combinations and debug bands need care

Opioids share an overdose burden. Each active exposure is divided by that route’s own overdose limit. The family syndrome can trigger when the sum is greater than 1.05. For example, 100 mcg of active IV fentanyl plus 5 mg of active IV morphine gives:

100 ÷ 150 + 5 ÷ 12 ≈ 1.083

This exceeds the shared trigger even though each drug alone is below its own trigger. Sedatives can also impair breathing below that threshold. The arithmetic describes current effect at those exposures, not an immediate prediction after injection.

Display bands do not always control toxicity. The generalized debug bands for epinephrine, norepinephrine, ketamine, opioids, midazolam, ondansetron, TXA, ceftriaxone and magnesium are display-only. Their actual effects use other code paths. In contrast, lidocaine, esmolol and calcium have concentration proxies that do drive specific hazards. Do not treat every green debug band as a safe-dose certificate.

Rate reductions take time. Fast infusion penalties use an onset and offset, and drugs already received continue through their own effect curves. Stop escalating simply because the last adjustment has not yet reached its peak.

Worked infusion example

50 mg ketamine added to 100 mL saline, delivered at 60 mL/h. This is a game arithmetic example.

  1. Draw the medication50 mg ÷ 50 mg/mL = 1 mL
  2. Include the injection volume100 + 1 = 101 mL
  3. Find the final concentration50 mg ÷ 101 mL ≈ 0.495 mg/mL
  4. Calculate delivered medication0.495 × 60 ≈ 29.7 mg/hAbout 0.495 mg/min, before leakage or stopped flow.

Use the drip calculator with the final bag concentration, then confirm actual drainage and clinical effect. With a 20 gtt/mL set, 60 mL/h requires 20 gtt/min before flow limits and complications. An intact bag draining at that rate takes 101 minutes to empty in the example.

The default clamp changes in 5 gtt/min increments. A requested dose rate may therefore fall between available settings. Site leakage also reduces the systemic dose. When multiple medications share a bag, one clamp changes every component’s rate together; it cannot independently titrate each medication. Do not assume that menu acceptance establishes chemical compatibility.

Pressors, sedatives and opioids still have adverse effects and interactions when infused. Delivery stopping does not instantly remove drug already received. Keep a working airway plan, reassess sedation and ventilation, and check the infusion site during PFC and transport.
