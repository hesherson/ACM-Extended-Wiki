Bleeding and shock

How blood loss changes preload, stroke volume, oxygen delivery and compensation. Use the trends alongside bleeding control, pulse, blood pressure and mental status.

01

Hemorrhage classes I to IV

These conventional classes describe the proportion of circulating volume lost. The amounts below use the mod’s 6 L reference. Classes organize assessment; they do not trigger a fixed heart rate, blood pressure, SpO₂ or DO₂ value.

ClassBlood lossAt a 6 L baselineCirculatory response and oxygen delivery
ILess than 15%Less than 0.9 LVenous return begins to fall. Compensatory vascular tone and rate can preserve the pressure reading. Delivery can decline before obvious hypotension.
II15% to 30%0.9 to 1.8 LLess ventricular filling reduces stroke volume. Tachycardia partly supports output; vasoconstriction supports pressure. SpO₂ can remain normal because the remaining hemoglobin is still well saturated.
IIIMore than 30% to 40%More than 1.8 to 2.4 LReduced filling overwhelms compensation. Low output and reduced tissue perfusion become more prominent. Positive pressure ventilation imposes an additional filling penalty.
IVMore than 40%More than 2.4 LSevere filling deficit threatens perfusion. Acidosis, poor myocardial oxygen supply and loss of effective rate can compound deterioration. A falling rate with worsening perfusion is concerning.

At the instant of proportional whole blood loss, red cells and plasma leave together, so hemoglobin concentration need not fall immediately. Total circulating red cell mass and cardiac filling still fall. Subsequent fluid shifts and non-red-cell replacement can dilute the remaining cells.

Class definitions: Hemorrhagic Shock, NCBI Bookshelf. The 6 L amounts and graph calculations use the game baseline.

02

Stroke volume, vasoconstriction and systemic vascular resistance

TermMeaningImplementation in ACM Extended
Preload and venous returnBlood returning to the heart supplies its filling before a beat.Approximated from circulating volume. The DO₂ calculation uses blood + saline + plasma, divides by the 6 L baseline and raises the ratio to 1.6 to estimate stroke volume. It does not calculate venous pressure or a separate venous reservoir.
Stroke volume (SV)Blood ejected by a ventricle per effective beat.The DO₂ estimate falls as volume falls and adds a filling penalty during active positive pressure ventilation. The native pressure pathway uses a separate volume based estimate with a 95 mL reference stroke volume. The two estimates are not interchangeable.
Cardiac output (CO)Blood flow generated over time by heart rate and stroke volume.Both reviewed pathways combine filling and rate. DO₂ uses a damped rate multiplier, capped at 1.35, with an extra low volume filling penalty above 150 BPM. The native pressure pathway uses its own rate and volume calculation.
VasoconstrictionNarrowing of vessels that increases resistance to flow.Blood loss drives a compensatory resistance adjustment in the native vital sign update. Medication and circulation systems add their effects to the same resistance calculation. Vessel diameter is not individually simulated.
Systemic vascular resistance (SVR)Resistance across the body's circulation.A combined peripheral resistance value contributes to blood pressure. Pressors, sedation, shock, calcium deficit, brain injury and other active systems can modify it. This is a game scale, not a clinical SVR value in measured units.
AfterloadThe load the ventricle must overcome to eject blood.Represented indirectly by pressure and vascular resistance. There is no separate afterload input that reduces stroke volume in either reviewed flow calculation. Increased resistance can raise the cuff reading without improving the DO₂ estimate.
ContractilityContraction strength at a given filling state.Relevant effects are approximated through other systems. Calcium deficit reduces vascular tone and pressor response; acidosis blunts pressure support. Neither reviewed flow calculation contains an independent contraction strength variable or a ventricular pressure volume simulation.
Atrial kick and coronary perfusionUpper chamber contraction assists filling; coronary vessels supply the heart muscle.The reviewed DO₂ and native output calculations do not include separate atrial contraction or coronary flow inputs. Use the actual rhythm, pressure, pulse and treatment rules in the rhythm reference.

Implementation checked on 13 September 2026: DO₂ and preload, native output, resistance composition, blood pressure, and circulation and calcium effects.

Pressure example: ignoring right atrial pressure, 5 L/min at a resistance of 16 mmHg·min/L gives MAP 80 mmHg. Output falling to 2.5 L/min with resistance rising to 32 gives the same MAP. The example illustrates why the cuff must be interpreted with perfusion findings.

The game uses separate calculations for blood pressure and normalized oxygen delivery. Its peripheral resistance value is not a clinical SVR measurement. The DO₂ calculation does not infer flow from MAP, so raising pressure with a pressor does not automatically restore the delivery estimate. See cardiology and delivery and Poiseuille’s law.

03

Pressure and ongoing hemorrhage

The default pressure factor is MAP ÷ 70, limited to 0.55× through 2×. It modifies bleeding that is still occurring in the wound pathway that uses pressure. Lower pressure reduces that component of blood loss but also risks inadequate perfusion. The custom junctional channel separately reads systemic resistance and compensation; this graph does not predict its total rate.

Control the source and replace lost volume as indicated by the casualty’s condition. A pressure associated slowing of an uncontrolled bleed does not mean the wound is treated. TBI perfusion requirements now depend on injury severity. Stable mild and moderate injuries can recover in their normal recovery band; severe and critical structural injury retain reduced reserve and additional CPP requirements. See Traumatic Brain Injury.

High pressure can also increase the chance that a vulnerable clot reopens. That is a separate process from the ongoing bleeding multiplier shown above.

04

Oxygen delivery during resuscitation

Hemorrhage reduces the flow component of oxygen delivery. Non-red-cell fluid may improve filling while diluting oxygen carrying content; blood restores both. Lung injury or altitude can also reduce saturation, multiplying the remaining deficit.

The oxygen delivery guide includes numerical examples, a calculator for the current game model, the effect of PEEP, and the links to dysrhythmias, coronary perfusion and myocardial function.

04

Hypothermia & Active Rewarming (HPMK)

Hypothermia is the leg of the triad you can act on directly in the field, and the HPMK is the tool for it.

Typepassive external
Wrap time2 s
Unwrap time1 s
Consumedno, reusable

It is passive rewarming. The kit retains the casualty's own body heat and lets them drift their core temperature back up. That means it only works while they are still generating heat. An unstable or non-perfusing casualty is not producing enough to retain, so the HPMK does close to nothing for them, which is clinically correct: that patient needs active rewarming, and warmed fluid is the lever you have.

The kit is never consumed. It leaves your hands while deployed and is handed back when it comes off.

Two states, two levels of access

How far the kit is closed decides what you can still reach on the casualty, which is the whole trade-off: heat retention against access.

StateAvailableGated off
UnfoldedChest and left armEverything else on the body
Fully wrappedHead selection only: pupils, responsiveness, airway adjuncts, BVM, BVM with oxygen, non-rebreather, head injury management. Plus Unwrap HPMK.All body and limb treatments

Wrapping requires the casualty prone, supine or unconscious. You cannot wrap someone standing up.

A wrapped casualty who gets up under their own power sheds the blanket where they stand, and it is left on the ground as a pickable kit. Being dragged or carried does not shed it. That is worth knowing before you wrap someone who might wake up and walk.

Red Flags

  • Chasing a normal blood pressure with uncontrolled bleeding. You will pop clots you had already formed.
  • Transfusing without calcium. Each unit costs ionised calcium, which costs clotting and myocardial function.
  • Treating hypothermia as a comfort problem. Cold blood does not clot, and the effect multiplies with the other two legs.
  • A heart rate that is falling on its own. The compensation is failing, and arrest usually follows.
  • Head injury plus hemorrhage. Follow oxygen delivery and the injury’s pressure requirements together. Severe structural TBI needs CPP at least 55 mmHg; critical structural TBI needs at least 60 mmHg. Normal SpO₂ does not establish adequate brain perfusion.
06

Junctional Haemorrhage & AAJT-S

Five states, each a distinct step: open, packed, wrapped, and XStat. Bleeding falls as you progress. The AAJT-S applies with a pause, and Combat Gauze is tracked separately.

Two application sites, and the one you need depends on where the bleed is. Inguinal for groin and proximal thigh, axillary for a shoulder or high arm wound that a limb tourniquet cannot reach.

TBI and junctional bleeding

The brain’s autonomic response now changes the resistance used by the custom junctional flow calculation. The direction below holds other wound, volume and treatment factors constant.

TBI stateVascular responseEffect on an uncontrolled junctional bleed
Stable injuryTBI tone stays near neutralExisting wound, blood volume, resistance and treatment factors determine flow.
Compensated rising ICPPositive sympathetic tone increases peripheral resistanceHigher resistance reduces the custom flow contribution. A slower bleed does not mean the wound is controlled.
Exhausted autonomic reserveTone becomes labile and can turn negative, lowering resistanceFlow can increase as vascular support fails. Reassess bleeding and perfusion even if the full Cushing pattern never appeared.

Autonomic integrity also scales the wound’s temporary spasm and plug response. Full integrity preserves 100% of its ordinary compensatory ability. The configured zero-integrity floor is 25%; the current controller stops at 0.05 integrity, which leaves 28.75%. These are percentages of compensation available, not percentages of blood lost or a fixed multiplier applied to every TBI wound.

Signed tone reaches the junctional equation through systemic resistance only once. The integrity adjustment changes the separate temporary response. The custom loss is then included in the normal circulation update, so it does not create a second blood volume debit.

Control the wound while restoring volume and oxygen delivery. Follow acute injury, CPP and autonomic trends; pressure support alone does not restore missing red cells or stop bleeding.

Direct pressure over a fracture

Pressure over a fractured limb adds severe pain and can immediately wake an eligible lightly unconscious or obtunded patient. The wake-up check requires stable vital signs and no forced unconsciousness condition. It does not add artificial wounds or fracture damage. The patient remains lying after waking.
