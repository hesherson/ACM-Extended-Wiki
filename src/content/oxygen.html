Oxygen delivery

Why a casualty can have normal SpO₂ and still receive too little oxygen at the tissues. Hemorrhage affects filling and flow; dilution affects oxygen carrying content; altitude and lung disease affect saturation.

01

Content × flow

DO₂Oxygen delivery=Cardiac outputBlood flow×Arterial oxygen contentOxygen carried in the blood

Physiology: cardiac output is HR × stroke volume. Arterial oxygen content depends mainly on hemoglobin concentration and the fraction saturated with oxygen, plus a small dissolved component. SpO₂ estimates saturation; it does not measure circulating hemoglobin mass or blood flow.

In ACM Extended: delivery is a fraction of a healthy reference. It combines a blood content estimate, SpO₂ and a flow estimate based on volume and heart rate. A value of 100% means the default model reference, not a clinical oxygen delivery measurement in mL/min.

For the class definitions and the circulation terms, see hemorrhage classes and stroke volume and SVR.

02

SpO₂ during hypovolemia and poor perfusion

A normal saturation can coexist with severe shock. SpO₂ describes the oxygen saturation of the hemoglobin being sampled. It does not tell you how much blood remains, how much hemoglobin is circulating, or how much oxygen reaches the tissues each minute.

Saturation

How full are the available oxygen binding sites? The remaining blood can stay well saturated when the airway and lungs still work.

Oxygen content

How much oxygen can that blood carry? Less hemoglobin means less oxygen content at the same saturation. Losing red cells and plasma together can initially preserve concentration while total circulating volume falls.

Oxygen delivery

How much reaches tissue per minute? Hemorrhage reduces filling and flow. A high saturation does not replace that lost flow.

Physiology background: OpenStax, oxygen transport and hemoglobin saturation. The model examples below show the separate effect of blood volume and flow.

Why the probe becomes less reliable

Pulse oximetry also needs a usable pulsatile signal at the probe. Poor circulation and cold skin can reduce reading reliability. During hypovolemia, peripheral vasoconstriction and reduced perfusion can therefore make the signal weak or unavailable. A displayed number may be misleading; there is no dependable rule that every poor perfusion error points downward.

Calculation and limits

Circulating volume is blood + saline + plasma. The preload ratio is circulating volume ÷ 6 L, capped at 1.1. Estimated SV is that ratio raised to 1.6, with the positive pressure penalty applied when relevant.

The rate multiplier starts at 1 + 0.4 × (HR ÷ 75 − 1), limited to 0.3 through 1.35. Above 150 BPM, an additional filling penalty increases toward 220 BPM and scales with the volume deficit. These are game approximations, not a universal human Frank–Starling curve.

Relative hemoglobin concentration is blood ÷ (blood + effective saline dilution + plasma), capped at 1. Delivery is this fraction × SV estimate × rate multiplier × (SpO₂ ÷ 97). The calculator uses freshly added saline, so effective dilution equals the saline input.

In the running game, saline’s modeled dilution contribution has a 20-minute half-life and follows changes in the saline compartment. That decay does not itself remove the saline volume. It is separate from fluid volume conversion. Plasma does not use this saline dilution decay.

The native blood pressure calculation has a separate cardiac output approximation. This calculator reproduces the DO₂ estimate and cannot predict exact cuff readings, rhythm transitions, drug responses or arrest.

06

Positive pressure and preload

Raising pressure inside the chest opposes venous return. A casualty with reduced circulating volume has less reserve to tolerate that change. PEEP keeps pressure elevated between breaths and can increase the filling cost.

For the 35%-loss example, PPV at PEEP 5 reduces the remaining SV estimate by about 11.5%; PEEP 15 reduces it by about 17.2%. The reduction multiplies what is left of SV, rather than subtracting those points from a normal SV of 100%.

Correct hemorrhage and inadequate oxygenation together. The model includes the consequences of delayed oxygenation as well as the filling cost of ventilation. Reassess pulse, pressure, chest findings and oxygenation after starting support or changing PEEP.

08

Tissue delivery and brain injury

The default delivery model sets a general critical reference below 50% and a recovery reference at 58%, with a 45-second reperfusion window. Head injury defaults use a higher delivery threshold of 70%, a severe threshold of 35% and a clearing reference of 78%. These values guide the surrounding physiology systems; they are not an automatic countdown to arrest or a guarantee of recovery.

Mission options and other active systems affect the final response. Read the patient’s clinical findings, bleeding and trends instead of treating an isolated model threshold as a diagnosis.
